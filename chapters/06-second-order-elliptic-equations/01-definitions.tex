\section{Definitions}
\label{sec:6-1}
\subsection{Two forms of an elliptic operator}
The divergence form and nondivergence form are, respectively,
\begin{align}
 Lu&=-\partial_j(a^{ij}\partial_i u)+b^i\partial_i u+cu,
       \label{eq:6-div}\\
 Ku&=-a^{ij}\partial_{ij}u+\widetilde b^i\partial_i u+cu.
       \label{eq:6-nondiv}
\end{align}
Uniform ellipticity means
\[
 \theta|\xi|^2\leq a^{ij}(x)\xi_i\xi_j
 \quad(x\in U,\ \xi\in\R^n).
\]
For measurable coefficients this is required almost everywhere.
Boundedness of the entries gives an upper bound $\Lambda|\xi|^2$.
It is the principal part, not the lower-order terms, that defines ellipticity.

\Needspace{45mm}
\PDEAnnotation{散度型与非散度型}{散度型可把一个导数移到测试函数上，因而适合弱解。只有系数足够可微时，才能展开散度并把系数导数并入漂移项。}
If $A$ is $C^1$, the two formulas agree precisely when
$\widetilde b^i=b^i-\partial_j a^{ij}$.
If $A$ is merely bounded measurable, this conversion is not a
pointwise identity between ordinary functions.
Moreover, symmetry of $A$ does not imply symmetry of the full operator:
the drift $b\cdot Du$ generally destroys self-adjointness.

For example, $A=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)$ has
$\theta=1$ and $\Lambda=4$; the principal quadratic form has elliptical
level sets. In contrast, $u_{xx}-u_{yy}$ has a principal form of both
signs and is not elliptic. If the smallest eigenvalue of $A(x)$ approaches
zero, pointwise positive definiteness alone gives no uniform $\theta$.

\subsection{Weak solutions and the boundary condition}
Assume $A,b,c\in L^\infty(U)$ and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:6-bilinear}
 B[u,v]=\int_U\big(a^{ij}u_i v_j+b^i u_i v+cuv\big)\,dx,
 \qquad u,v\in H^1_0(U).
\end{equation}
A weak solution of $Lu=f$, with zero Dirichlet boundary values, is
$u\in H^1_0(U)$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:6-weak}
 B[u,v]=\langle f,v\rangle\quad(v\in H^1_0(U)).
\end{equation}
Here $f\in H^{-1}(U)$; in particular $f\in L^2(U)$ is allowed.

\Needspace{45mm}
\PDEAnnotation{零边界的准确含义}{$H^1_0(U)$ 是紧支撑光滑函数在 $H^1$ 中的闭包。光滑边界上它等价于零迹；一般弱解没有逐点边界值，更不能先假设法向导数存在。}
For smooth $u$ with $u=0$ on the boundary, integration by parts in the
principal term gives \eqref{eq:6-weak}. Conversely, testing against
$C^\infty_c(U)$ shows that \eqref{eq:6-weak} implies $Lu=f$ as a
distribution. If $u$ subsequently belongs to $C^2(U)$ and the
coefficients and $f$ are continuous in the relevant form, the
distributional equation becomes the classical equation.

A useful description of rough data is
$f=f_0-\operatorname{div}F$, with $f_0,F_i\in L^2(U)$. Its action is
\[
 \langle f,v\rangle=\int_U(f_0v+F\cdot Dv)\,dx,
 \qquad
 |\langle f,v\rangle|\leq C(\|f_0\|_2+\|F\|_2)\|v\|_{H^1}.
\]
The plus sign in the second term comes from distributional differentiation.

\Needspace{45mm}
\PDEAnnotation{非齐次边界先做提升}{若边界数据是某个 $w\in H^1(U)$ 的迹，就写 $u=w+z$，再解 $z\in H^1_0(U)$。边界数据仅属于 $L^2(\partial U)$，并不自动保证存在这样的提升。}
For prescribed trace $g=Tw$ with $w\in H^1(U)$, solve
\[
 B[z,v]=\langle f,v\rangle-B[w,v]\quad(v\in H^1_0(U)),
 \qquad z=u-w\in H^1_0(U).
\]
Thus the homogeneous theory applies whenever a lift is available.
The resulting $u$ is independent of the chosen lift whenever the
homogeneous problem is unique.

\paragraph{Adjoint and sign conventions.}
Define $B^*[v,u]=B[u,v]$. For smooth coefficients, the formal adjoint is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:6-adjoint}
 L^*v=-\partial_i(a^{ij}\partial_jv)-\partial_i(b^i v)+cv.
\end{equation}
The adjoint drift differentiates the product $b^i v$.
In particular, even if $c\geq0$, the expanded zeroth-order coefficient
of $L^*$ can be negative. This matters in the nonsymmetric spectral theory.
