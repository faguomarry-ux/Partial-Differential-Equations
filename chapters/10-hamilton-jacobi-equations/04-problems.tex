\section{Problems}
\label{sec:10-4}
The descriptions below retain the source numbers 1--14. All solutions are
editorial reconstructions. The viscosity convention is stated whenever the
time direction or the sign of the equation changes.

\subsection*{1. Negating the unknown changes the Hamiltonian}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
Let $w=-u$ and $\widehat H(p,x)=-H(-p,x)$. If $w-\phi$ has an
upper contact, $u-(-\phi)$ has a lower contact. The supersolution
inequality for $u$ reads
$-\phi_t+H(-D\phi,x)\geq0$, exactly
$\phi_t+\widehat H(D\phi,x)\leq0$. Lower contacts reverse the reasoning.
Boundedness and uniform continuity are preserved, as are initial traces
with $g$ replaced by $-g$. Negating the unknown interchanges upper and
lower contacts; multiplying a PDE expression by $-1$ while leaving the
unknown and the test inequalities unchanged does not do this.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{2. Uniform stability for a first-order equation}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
At a strict upper contact for the limit, maximize $u_k-\phi$ on a small
closed contact ball. Uniform convergence puts the maxima in its interior
and makes their points converge to the original contact point. Apply
$\phi_t+H(D\phi,x)\leq0$ there and use continuity of $H$.
Strictification and lower contacts give the full PDE. A uniform limit
on each full closed slab of BUC functions remains BUC and inherits the
limiting initial trace. If only local uniform convergence is known,
the local PDE conclusion still holds, but membership in the global BUC
class must be checked separately. The same contact proof works for
$H_k\to H$ locally uniformly in $(p,x)$: the test gradients stay in a
compact set even when the unknown functions have no gradient bounds.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{3. Anisotropic positive diffusion selects the same notion}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
Let the fixed smooth matrix $A=(a_{ij})$ have nonnegative symmetric part
(the source assumes uniform ellipticity). At a smooth upper contact,
$D^2u^\varepsilon\leq D^2\phi$, and hence
\[
 \phi_t+H(D\phi,x)
 =\varepsilon\operatorname{tr}(A D^2u^\varepsilon)
 \leq\varepsilon\operatorname{tr}(A D^2\phi).
\]
To see the trace order, for positive semidefinite symmetric $A,B$,
$\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(A^{1/2}BA^{1/2})\geq0$;
the antisymmetric part has zero contraction with a symmetric Hessian.
The contact points converge, $A$ and $D^2\phi$ are bounded locally,
and the right side tends to zero. Lower contacts yield the reverse
inequality. Boundary traces and the BUC class are treated as in Problem 2.
Uniform ellipticity helps construct approximants but is stronger than
the positivity needed in this limit argument.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{4. Sup-norm contraction}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
Set $m=\sup_x|g_1-g_2|$. Since $H$ contains no zeroth-order value
variable, $u_2+m$ remains a supersolution and $g_1\leq g_2+m$.
Comparison gives $u_1\leq u_2+m$. Interchange indices to obtain the
opposite inequality. Thus $\|u_1(\cdot,t)-u_2(\cdot,t)\|_\infty\leq m$
on every finite slab. There is no requirement that either supremum
be attained at a finite spatial point.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{5. The sign of the eikonal equation matters}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
For $u=1-|x|$, every nonzero point has slope $\pm1$, so the equation
holds classically there. If a smooth $\phi$ touches from above at $0$,
the right and left one-sided inequalities give
$-1\leq\phi'(0)\leq1$. Thus $|\phi'(0)|-1\leq0$.
There is no smooth lower test at $0$: it would require both
$\phi'(0)\leq-1$ from the right and $\phi'(0)\geq1$ from the left.
Hence the lower-test condition is vacuous there. This proves (a).

For $\tilde u=|x|-1$, the constant lower test $\phi=-1$ at zero
violates $|\phi'(0)|-1\geq0$, proving (b).
For (c), use instead $F(p)=1-|p|$. The lower-test slopes for $\tilde u$
lie in $[-1,1]$, so $F\geq0$, and it has no upper test at its upward
corner. Away from zero, $F=0$. Thus $\tilde u$ solves the sign-reversed
equation in its own viscosity sense. For (d), the two residuals have the
same classical zero set, but their upper- and lower-test inequalities
are different. They encode opposite choices at a corner, exactly as
Diagram T10.1 illustrates.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{6. Distance to the boundary is an eikonal solution}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
For any nonempty bounded open $U$, $d(x)=\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial U)$
is $1$-Lipschitz by the triangle inequality; no boundary smoothness is
required. At an upper smooth contact its gradient has norm at most one
by the Lipschitz contact estimate proved in \S10.2.
At a lower contact $x_0\in U$, choose a nearest boundary point $y$,
possible because $\partial U$ is closed and bounded. Set
$e=(y-x_0)/d(x_0)$. For $0<h<d(x_0)$ the point $x_0+he$ is inside
$U$, and
\[
 d(x_0+he)=d(x_0)-h.
\]
The upper bound uses the same $y$; the lower bound uses the Lipschitz
estimate. Lower touching gives
$\phi(x_0+he)-\phi(x_0)\leq-h$, hence $D\phi(x_0)\cdot e\leq-1$.
Therefore $|D\phi(x_0)|\geq1$, proving the supersolution condition.
The continuous extension of $d$ vanishes at the boundary.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{7. Admissible gradient jumps at a smooth interface}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
Continuity of $u$ and smoothness of its traces force the tangential
components of $Du^+$ and $Du^-$ to agree on $\Gamma$.
At a fixed point use smooth local coordinates $(z,\rho)$, where
$\rho$ increases into $V^+$ and $\partial_\rho=\nu$ at that point.
If $u_\nu^-<u_\nu^+$, choose any number $a$ strictly between them.
For a sufficiently small neighborhood the smooth function
\[
 \phi(z,\rho)=u(z,0)+a\rho
\]
is a lower test along the interface: on $\rho>0$ the normal derivative
of $u$ exceeds $a$, while on $\rho<0$ it is less than $a$.
Integrating those derivative inequalities from zero gives $u\geq\phi$
on both sides. Its gradient at the point is the common tangential
gradient plus $a\nu$. The viscosity lower-test condition gives
$H(\lambda Du^-+(1-\lambda)Du^+)\geq0$ for $0<\lambda<1$.
The endpoint values of $H$ are zero by the classical equation on each
side and continuity up to $\Gamma$; thus the closed interval follows.
If the normal derivatives agree, both full gradients agree and the same
endpoint argument suffices. For a downward jump the function $\phi$
is an upper test, giving $H\leq0$ on the segment. This proves both
source inequalities, including equality cases.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{8. Shape from shading}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
For the graph $(x_1,x_2,u(x))$, the upward unit normal is
$\nu=(-Du,1)/\sqrt{1+|Du|^2}$. Hence the normalized Lambertian
intensity is $i=e_3\cdot\nu=(1+|Du|^2)^{-1/2}$.
For $0<i(x)\leq1$ this gives the eikonal equation
\[
 |Du|=n(x):=\sqrt{i(x)^{-2}-1}.
\]
If $i=1$ the slope is zero; if $i\downarrow0$ the required finite-graph
slope diverges. Values outside $(0,1]$ do not fit this normalized graph
model. The PDE determines a slope magnitude, not a surface without
boundary data and a solution-selection condition.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{9. Pure pursuit and an exact interception time}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
Use relative coordinates $x_1$ for the target's horizontal displacement
from the pursuer and $x_2$ for the pursuer's height above the target.
Writing $r=\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2}$, the relative dynamics before interception are
\[
 x_1'=b_1-b_2x_1/r,\qquad x_2'=-b_2x_2/r.
\]
The remaining-time identity $u(x)=h+u(X(h))$ gives
\[
 (b_1-b_2x_1/r)u_{x_1}-(b_2x_2/r)u_{x_2}=-1,\qquad u(0,0)=0.
\]
As an editorial check the exact solution is
\[
 u(x_1,x_2)={b_2\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2}+b_1x_1\over b_2^2-b_1^2}.
\]
Indeed $r'=b_1x_1/r-b_2$, so differentiating the numerator along a
trajectory gives $-(b_2^2-b_1^2)$. Also $r'\leq b_1-b_2<0$ ensures
finite interception, and the displayed expression tends to zero there.
Thus its constant rate of decrease $-1$ identifies it as the actual time.
On $x_2=0$, $x_1>0$ it reduces to $x_1/(b_2-b_1)$, as expected.
The transport field is singular at interception; the PDE is used only
away from $r=0$ and the zero-time value is imposed by continuity.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{10. Discount and regularity on an infinite horizon}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
For $\lambda>0$ define
$V(x)=\inf_\alpha\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda s}r(X(s),\alpha(s))ds$.
Since $|r|\leq R$, $|V|\leq R/\lambda$. For the same control at $x,y$,
the running-cost difference is at most
$\min\{L_re^{L_fs}|x-y|,2R\}$. If $\lambda>L_f$, integrate its first
bound to get
$|V(x)-V(y)|\leq L_r|x-y|/(\lambda-L_f)$.
If $0<\lambda\leq L_f$, choose any $0<\alpha<\lambda/L_f$
(and hence $\alpha<1$). For nonnegative $a,b$,
$\min(a,b)\leq a^\alpha b^{1-\alpha}$, so
\[
 |V(x)-V(y)|\leq
 {L_r^\alpha(2R)^{1-\alpha}\over\lambda-\alpha L_f}|x-y|^\alpha.
\]
The estimates are uniform in the control, so passing to infima preserves
them. The case $R=0$ or $L_r=0$ is immediate. If $L_f=0$, every
$\lambda>0$ is in the Lipschitz case. At $\lambda=L_f$ the stated
argument gives every $\alpha<1$, without asserting Lipschitz continuity.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{11. The discounted stationary Bellman equation}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
Restriction and concatenation, with the discount factored out of the
tail, give
\[
 V(x)=\inf_\alpha\left\{\int_0^h e^{-\lambda s}r(X(s),\alpha(s))ds
                  +e^{-\lambda h}V(X(h))\right\}.
\]
At a normalized upper contact $V-\phi$, choose a constant control $a$.
The expression on the right for this control is at least $V(x)$;
replace $V(X(h))$ by its upper test and expand to first order:
$0\leq r(x,a)+f(x,a)\cdot D\phi(x)-\lambda V(x)$.
This holds for every $a$, so
\[
 \lambda V(x)-\min_a\{f(x,a)\cdot D\phi(x)+r(x,a)\}\leq0.
\]
At a lower contact choose an $h^2$-optimal prefix, use the lower test,
and the uniform continuity in $a$ argument of \S10.3. Division by $h$
now gives the reverse inequality. Thus $V$ is a viscosity solution of
$\lambda V-H_c(DV,x)=0$ with the usual stationary convention.
The positive coefficient of $V$ is produced by differentiating the
discount factor, not by inserting an additional running cost.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{12. Degenerate ellipticity and extrema of operators}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
Write $R\geq S$ when $R-S$ is positive semidefinite.
Then $-\operatorname{tr}R\leq-\operatorname{tr}S$.
For symmetric $A\geq0$,
$\operatorname{tr}(A(R-S))=\operatorname{tr}(A^{1/2}(R-S)A^{1/2})\geq0$,
so $F(R)=-A:R$ is elliptic in the source's decreasing-matrix convention.
If every $F_k(R,p,x)\leq F_k(S,p,x)$, taking a finite maximum or a
finite minimum preserves that inequality. Consequently both Bellman-type
maxima and minima of such linear operators are degenerate elliptic.
Strict positive definiteness is not needed for these order statements.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{13. Classical consistency for second-order equations}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
Let $u\in C^2$ solve $F(D^2u,Du,x)=0$ with $F$ elliptic as in
Problem 12. At an upper smooth contact, $Du=D\phi$ and
$D^2u\leq D^2\phi$, so
\[
 F(D^2\phi,D\phi,x)\leq F(D^2u,Du,x)=0.
\]
At a lower contact both the matrix order and the resulting inequality
reverse. This proves viscosity consistency. The ellipticity sign is
essential: it is exactly what connects Hessian order at a contact to
the direction of the PDE inequality.
\end{proof}

\subsection*{14. Uniform stability for second-order equations}
\begin{proof}[Solution]
Assume, as in the preceding problem, that $F$ is continuous and elliptic,
and that $u_k\to u$ locally uniformly. Strictify an upper contact by
adding $\delta|x-x_0|^4$ to $\phi$. This preserves both the gradient
and Hessian at $x_0$. Interior maxima of $u_k-\phi_\delta$ converge
to $x_0$. At those points use the viscosity subsolution inequality and
pass to the limit in the continuous $F$, including the derivatives of
the fixed test. At $x_0$ the quartic perturbation contributes no first
or second derivative, giving the desired original-test inequality.
Strictify a lower contact by subtracting the quartic and repeat.
This is a stability proof; it does not prove existence or comparison
for general fully nonlinear second-order equations.
\end{proof}
